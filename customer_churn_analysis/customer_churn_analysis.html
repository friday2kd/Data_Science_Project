Customer Churn Prediction: Explained and Bias-Aware Analysis¶

Business problem: Which customers are at higher risk of leaving, and how should a business prioritise follow-up? This is a telecommunications dataset; the techniques may transfer to energy retail but these results are not evidence about energy customers.

Research questions: (1) How common is churn? (2) Which recorded factors correlate with churn? (3) Can models rank customers more effectively than a no-skill baseline? (4) Do prediction errors differ across customer groups?

Interpretation rule: Prediction is not causation. A high predicted risk does not show that an incentive would prevent churn. The dataset is observational and cross-sectional; we cannot estimate retention intervention effects from it.

Reproducibility: Execute cells in order in Jupyter. Keep raw data out of GitHub unless its redistribution licence permits publication. Record Python/package versions and the source/licence in your README.

Stage 2: Exploratory data analysis (EDA)¶

Why: Understand the business and detect data issues before modelling. Compare observed churn rates across contract types and tenure bands.

How to interpret: A group with 40% churn has a higher observed proportion of churn than one with 20%; it does not mean contract type caused that difference. Other factors such as tenure or billing may explain the association.

Bias control: Show group sizes alongside rates. Very small groups produce unstable estimates. Avoid cherry-picking patterns; document hypotheses and inspect all important categories, including missing/unknown values.

Findings: contract type and customer tenure¶

The exploratory analysis found substantial differences in observed churn across contract types and customer tenure.

Customers on month-to-month contracts had the highest churn rate (about 43%), compared with about 11% for one-year contracts and 3% for two-year contracts. Contract type is strongly associated with retention, with longer contracts showing lower observed churn.

Customers who churned generally had shorter relationships with the company. Median tenure was about 10 months for churned customers, compared with 38 months for those who stayed.

Implications for modelling: Contract type and tenure are likely to be informative predictors. Their relationship with churn needs further testing through multivariable modelling, feature importance and interaction checks.

Business implications: There may be value in looking at early-stage engagement and retention for customers on flexible contracts. These associations should not be read as causal effects.

Limitations: This analysis is descriptive and does not adjust for confounders such as pricing, service type, demographics or the interaction between contract and tenure. Later stages add validation, subgroup checks and leakage controls.

Stage 3: Train/test separation and leakage-safe preprocessing¶

Why: We need an unbiased estimate of how the model may perform on unseen customers. Reserve 20% as a final test set; use only the other 80% for model selection.

What the code does: Stratifies by churn so both partitions have similar churn prevalence. Numerical fields use median imputation and scaling; categorical fields use most-frequent imputation and one-hot encoding. All transformations are fitted inside a pipeline, separately within each training fold.

Bias control: This avoids data leakage from test rows into training. customerID is excluded. Check for other variables that would only be known after churn or at the time of a retention decision. Random splitting cannot prove performance on future customers: real deployment requires a dated, forward-looking holdout.

Stage 4: Five-fold stratified cross-validation and model selection¶

Why: A single split can be lucky or unlucky. Five-fold CV repeatedly trains on four training folds and validates on the fifth, then averages the scores.

Models: DummyClassifier estimates a no-skill baseline; Logistic Regression is interpretable; Random Forest can capture nonlinear relationships.

Metrics: ROC-AUC measures ranking across thresholds. PR-AUC / average precision is particularly useful when the positive class (churn) is less common. F1 balances precision and recall at a fixed decision threshold. We select by training-only CV PR-AUC.

Bias control: Do not select models based on the test set. Report the variability across folds, not only the mean. Model comparison should use identical splits and preprocessing rules. If extensive hyperparameter tuning is added, use nested CV or a separate validation split.

import pandas as pd
from sklearn.base import clone
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_validate

cv = StratifiedKFold(
    n_splits=5,
    shuffle=True,
    random_state=42
)

scoring = {
    "ROC_AUC": "roc_auc",
    "PR_AUC": "average_precision",
    "F1": "f1"
}

results = []

for name, estimator in models.items():

    pipeline = Pipeline([
        ("prep", clone(preprocess)),
        ("model", clone(estimator))
    ])

    scores = cross_validate(
        pipeline,
        X_train,
        y_train,
        cv=cv,
        scoring=scoring,
        n_jobs=-1
    )

    row = {"Model": name}

    for metric in scoring:
        values = scores[f"test_{metric}"]
        row[f"{metric}_Mean"] = values.mean()
        row[f"{metric}_SD"] = values.std(ddof=1)

    results.append(row)

cv_summary = pd.DataFrame(results)
display(cv_summary.round(4))

Stage 5: Final untouched holdout evaluation¶

Why: The holdout approximates performance on customers not used to develop the model. Evaluate once after model selection.

Confusion matrix: True positive = correctly flagged churner; false positive = retained customer incorrectly flagged; false negative = churner missed; true negative = correctly identified retained customer.

Business trade-off: Higher recall finds more churners but may create more unnecessary outreach. The default 0.50 threshold is a demonstration, not an optimised business policy. Choose thresholds using training/validation data and estimated outreach costs, not by repeatedly examining the test set.

Bias control: Report both class-specific precision/recall and prevalence-sensitive PR-AUC. Check calibration before treating model scores as meaningful probabilities. A random holdout is not a substitute for out-of-time validation.

Stage 6: Explainability and feature importance¶

Why: Stakeholders need to understand which recorded inputs help the model make predictions.

Method: Permutation importance shuffles one feature in the holdout data and measures the change in average precision. A larger reduction indicates stronger predictive reliance.

Limitations: Importance is not causal. Correlated variables can mask one another; a predictive variable may be a proxy for an unrecorded factor. Treat the holdout explanation as descriptive, not a basis for retuning the model.

Stage 8: Probability calibration and subgroup error audit¶

Calibration: Among customers assigned about 70% risk, roughly 70% should actually churn in a well-calibrated sample. The Brier score measures probability error (lower is better). A calibration plot highlights mismatches; do not fit a calibrator on the final test set.

Subgroup diagnostics: Compare recall (fraction of actual churners detected) and false-positive rate (fraction of retained customers wrongly flagged) by recorded gender, SeniorCitizen, and Partner categories. We suppress rates for small groups (fewer than 30 total or fewer than 10 examples of either outcome). These are descriptive checks, not a fairness certification. Consider confidence intervals and legal/contextual review before any deployment.

Action if disparities appear: Check data quality and group representation; evaluate performance on new samples; compare threshold and calibration choices on validation data; investigate proxy features; document trade-offs and involve affected stakeholders. Never adjust a model solely to make a test-set fairness metric look better.

Stage 9: Limitations, fairness and responsible use¶

  • Selection/representation bias: This sample may not reflect a particular region, energy provider, or future customer base. Compare the deployment population with the training sample.
  • Label bias: Churn reflects recorded departures; verify its operational definition and whether outcomes are consistently captured.
  • Historical bias: Contract, payment and service variables may encode past business practices and unequal access.
  • Proxy discrimination: Removing gender or senior-citizen status alone does not guarantee fairness; other features may correlate with them.
  • Measurement bias: TotalCharges blanks and other missing values may not be random; document and test sensitivity to missingness.
  • Evaluation bias: A random holdout can overstate future performance; use time-based external validation when dated records become available.
  • Fairness: Compare group-level recall, false-positive rates, precision and calibration with uncertainty and minimum sample sizes. No single fairness metric proves a system is fair.
  • Causality: Observational associations do not justify claims about what will reduce churn. Test retention strategies prospectively.
  • Privacy: Use this public learning dataset for GitHub; do not upload proprietary or personally identifying customer data.

Next steps: Compare CV standard deviations; test probability calibration; predefine an outreach threshold using validation data; audit group-level error rates; validate on an out-of-time dataset; document a model card and monitoring plan.

Lowering the threshold from 50% to 35% raises churn recall by 17.4 points and F1 by 4.2 points. The model flags about a third of customers instead of a fifth, and more retained customers would receive unnecessary outreach.

Decision: Use 35% as the provisional F1-based threshold. For a real campaign, the final rule should depend on outreach capacity and cost. A team that can only contact 10% of customers may prefer the top 10% by risk rather than everyone above 35%.

Conclusion¶

This project developed and evaluated machine learning models for predicting telecommunications customer churn using 7,043 customer records.

Logistic Regression emerged as the preferred model, achieving a cross-validated ROC-AUC of 84.61% and Average Precision of 66.14%, outperforming Random Forest and the Dummy baseline.

Feature importance analysis identified customer tenure, internet service type, monthly charges and contract type as important predictors of churn.

Probability calibration analysis indicated that the original Logistic Regression model produced reasonably reliable risk estimates without requiring additional calibration.

Threshold optimisation improved validation churn recall from 54.3% to 71.7% and F1-score from 59.3% to 63.5% by reducing the classification threshold from 50% to 35%. A single holdout check confirmed the recall gain (55.9% to 70.6%), with a smaller F1 improvement (60.4% to 61.5%).

Overall, the findings demonstrate the potential of machine learning to support customer churn risk identification and retention prioritisation. However, further fairness evaluation, independent validation and real-world intervention testing are necessary before operational deployment.
